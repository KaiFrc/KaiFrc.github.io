01 — Background

A Prediction That Became a Promise

In 1965, Gordon Moore — then director of R&D at Fairchild Semiconductor — published a paper in Electronics magazine containing an observation that would define the next six decades of civilization. He noticed that the number of components on an integrated circuit had been doubling roughly every year, and predicted this trend would continue.

That observation became Moore's Law: a self-fulfilling prophecy the semiconductor industry adopted as both a roadmap and a competitive target. Companies like Intel, TSMC, and Samsung raced to outpace each other — and in doing so, collectively delivered on the prediction year after year, decade after decade.

"The complexity for minimum component costs has increased at a rate of roughly a factor of two per year." — Gordon Moore, Electronics Magazine, 1965

The consequences are almost incomprehensible. The Apollo Guidance Computer that landed humans on the moon had approximately 4,000 transistors. Today's Apple M4 contains 28 billion — on a piece of silicon smaller than a postage stamp. Your phone is not slightly more powerful than the machines that ran NASA. It is millions of times more powerful, for a fraction of the cost.

03 — The Wall

When Physics Says No

The MOSFET operates by using a gate voltage to control current through a channel. A thin oxide layer insulates the gate. As transistors shrank, this layer had to shrink too — maintaining the electrostatic control that makes the device work.

By the mid-2000s, gate oxide layers had reached just a few nanometers — roughly 10–15 atomic layers. At this scale, quantum tunneling dominates: electrons no longer wait for the transistor to switch — they pass through the barrier, creating leakage current even when the device is supposed to be off. The result: wasted power, excess heat, unreliable switching.

04 — Economics

Moore's Law Was Also an Economic Claim

Gordon Moore's observation was never purely physical — the full argument was that the cost per transistor would decrease exponentially as density increased. More transistors for less money, every two years. This dimension is where the law has arguably already collapsed.

1970s – 1990s

The Golden Era

Each new node delivered more transistors at lower cost per transistor. Moore's prediction held almost perfectly, funding a 30-year technology boom accessible to the world.

Early 2000s

The Clock Speed Wall

CPU frequencies plateaued around 3–4 GHz. Power density became the binding constraint. The industry pivoted to multi-core: more processors, not faster ones.

2015 – Present

The Cost Inversion

A leading-edge fab now costs $20 billion to build. EUV lithography equipment from ASML runs over $150 million per machine. Cost per transistor has stopped falling — and in some cases has risen.

2022

The CHIPS Act — $52 Billion

U.S. federal investment to rebuild domestic semiconductor manufacturing — an acknowledgment that Moore's Law had created a strategic vulnerability concentrated in a single island off the coast of China.

05 — What Comes Next

The Industry's Four Bets

No single technology succeeds Moore's Law. Instead, the semiconductor industry is placing parallel bets — each representing a different philosophy for continuing to improve computing performance.

Path 01

3D Stacking & Chiplets

Stack dies vertically rather than shrinking them. AMD's Ryzen uses chiplet tiles to achieve transistor counts impossible in a single monolithic die — build up, not out.

Path 02

New Materials: GaN & SiC

Wide-bandgap semiconductors operate at higher voltages and temperatures. GaN and SiC open performance spaces silicon cannot enter — powering EVs, 5G, and defense systems.

Path 03

Quantum Computing

Superposition and entanglement allow certain calculations to run exponentially faster. IBM's roadmap targets 100,000+ qubits — but fault-tolerant quantum advantage remains a research-stage goal.

Path 04

Neuromorphic Computing

Mimic biological neural networks with collocated computation and memory. Intel's Loihi 2 achieves orders of magnitude greater energy efficiency for AI inference than conventional silicon.

06 — Conclusion

The Law Is Dead.
The Progress Isn't.

As a physical claim that transistor density doubles every two years at constant cost, Moore's Law has effectively ended. The curve has bent. The economics have inverted. The physics has been exhausted at the atomic boundary.

But as a broader commitment to continuous improvement in computing capability, the spirit of Moore's Law persists — through radically different mechanisms. The future of computing is not a single successor technology. It is a portfolio of parallel bets, each more domain-specific, more expensive, and more geopolitically entangled than Moore's original vision.

These are not abstract engineering questions. Decisions made in the next decade about chip manufacturing, materials science, and quantum architecture will determine who controls AI, what electric vehicles can do, and how democracies navigate a world where the most critical infrastructure is built by a handful of companies on a contested island in the Pacific.

// Physically & Economically

Yes — it's over

Moore's Law as Gordon Moore defined it — exponential transistor density at falling cost — has ended. The industry acknowledged this privately years before publicly admitting it.

// As Aspiration

No — the work continues

Computing performance will keep improving — but through harder, costlier, and more specialized methods. The free lunch is over. The ambition remains.
