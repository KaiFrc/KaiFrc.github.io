Project 4 — Reflection

Process, Purpose,
and Mode

Developing the Question

The central question — "Is Moore's Law dead, and what happens to everything built on top of it?" — emerged from a tension I noticed in my own coursework. In my electronics courses, we study MOSFETs as the fundamental building block of all modern computing. We learn how gate oxide thickness and threshold voltage determine performance. But we rarely step back and ask: how long can this scaling actually continue? The question felt genuinely open-ended to me, which is what made it feel right for this project. It is not a question with a clean textbook answer.

I was also drawn to the fact that this question has stakes for non-engineers. Moore's Law is not just a technical fact — it is the foundation of economic planning, AI development timelines, and geopolitical strategy. Asking whether it is ending forces a broader audience to confront something they have probably taken for granted: that technology will keep getting better for free, forever.

Defining Audience and Purpose

My primary audience is my English Composition classmates — people who use technology daily but may not have a technical background. My secondary audience is anyone outside of engineering who is curious about where computing is headed. My purpose was not to teach a device physics lecture, but to explain a consequential shift happening in technology right now — and to argue that it matters to everyone, not just chip designers.

This audience shaped almost every decision I made. I led with the consequence (your phone is more powerful than Apollo mission hardware) before introducing the technical mechanism. I defined every technical term — gate oxide, process node, tunneling — in plain language before using it. I tried to make each section feel like it was answering a question a curious non-engineer might genuinely ask.

Modes of Communication

I chose to work with three modes: Linguistic, Visual, and Spatial.

Linguistic mode is the backbone of the composition — the written arguments, headings, pull quotes, and citations. I worked hard to vary the register of the language, moving between technical precision (when discussing quantum tunneling or process nodes) and accessible, grounded prose (when connecting the topic to broader stakes). The pull quotes from Moore and from the Nature article serve a dual linguistic and visual function — they give the reader a moment to pause and let a single idea land before the argument continues.

Visual mode operates through the data visualization (the transistor count graph), the color system, and the typographic choices. The graph was the most deliberate visual decision: an exponential curve on a logarithmic scale is not self-explanatory, so I paired it with a legend and contextual framing text that told the reader what to look for. The gold color palette was chosen to evoke printed circuit board traces — a subtle visual connection to the subject matter without being illustrative or literal.

Spatial mode governs the overall layout and navigation. I chose a single-page scrolling web composition because the spatial logic of scrolling — moving forward through an argument — mirrors the temporal progression of the story I was telling. The sticky navigation allows readers to locate themselves in the argument at any time, which is especially important for an audience that may not already have a mental map of the topic. The two-column layouts, stat cards, and timeline components each create a different spatial rhythm that signals a different kind of information to the reader.

I considered using audio mode — specifically, embedding links to relevant podcast episodes or video interviews with semiconductor engineers — but ultimately decided that an HTML composition with embedded audio would be harder to submit and evaluate in a Canvas context. Future iterations of this project would benefit from audio narration or embedded video segments.

What I Would Do Differently

If I had more time, I would have integrated audio more deliberately — perhaps a short recorded narration over the introductory section, or an embedded interview clip from an IEEE Spectrum podcast episode. I would also have gone deeper on the policy angle: the CHIPS Act and the Taiwan question deserve more than a paragraph. The question of who controls the future of semiconductor manufacturing is as much a political story as a technical one, and that complexity deserves its own section.

Overall, working in multiple modes changed how I thought about the argument itself. Having to represent data visually forced me to be more precise about the claims I was making. Thinking about spatial layout forced me to sequence the argument more deliberately. Writing for the web rather than a printed page made me think about where a reader's attention goes, and how to keep it.
